The High Line: How Green Gentrification Harms New Yorkers and their Ecosystems

by: Gaia Rueda Moreno

The High Society Line

Nearly two centuries of industrial development has allowed American cities, like New York, to expand in size both in terms of size and population. The ceaseless march of urbanization has naturally encroached upon green spaces, leading to habitat loss and fragmentation. These changes don’t just impact non-humans however, as these spaces help ameliorate air pollution, improve storm-water drainage, and combat the urban heat island effect1. In the Era of Anthropogenic Climate Change, the importance of these services is undeniable, and NYC’s urban planners have certainly caught on.

One of the Big Apple’s largest greening efforts came under the administration of Michael Bloomberg, whose ‘plaNYC 2030’ program sought to interlace economic revitalization with environmental concerns, with the goal of reimagining NYC as a “Luxury City”1. Enlisting the expertise of his Planning Commissioner Amanda Burden, they rezoned 40% of the city and constructed 730 acres of new parks. One of the most infamous of these new urban parks is the High Line, an elevated rail line which was redeveloped into a public park thanks to the creation of the West Chelsea Special District2. Stretching out from Hudson Yards down to the Meatpacking district, it features extensive gardens and seating spaces, as well as various art exhibitions and quaint market spaces3.
